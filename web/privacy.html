Privacy

Last updated 3 September 2026
Local-first session traffic

The iPhone and Watch apps include no analytics SDK or tracking cookies. LeSearch Mesh has no accounts and no LeSearch relay in the session data path. Your phone talks directly to machines you own; your Watch connects directly when possible or through its paired iPhone. The daemon's optional heartbeat and notification text passing through Apple's push service are each explained in full below.

Who this is

LeSearch Mesh is made by LeSearch AI (Arya Teja Rudraraju). Questions about any of this: aryayt55@gmail.com.

What the apps send, and to whom

When you pair a machine, the iPhone app stores that machine's address and the access token the machine itself minted. From then on the phone makes HTTP requests directly to that machine — its stats, its agent sessions, its terminal output, and the commands you send back. The Watch connects directly when it can; otherwise its paired iPhone polls the machine and sends snapshots over WatchConnectivity. Neither route uses a LeSearch relay.

That transport is currently plain HTTP with a bearer token. Use LeSearch Mesh only on a trusted private LAN or a VPN you already trust; it is not safe on a shared or public network until TLS ships.

The apps do not send your prompts, terminal output, hostnames, source code, or product usage analytics to LeSearch AI. They contain no analytics SDK or tracking cookies. The optional daemon heartbeat is described below; App Store privacy disclosures are reviewed separately for each released build.

The daemon's heartbeat

The meshd daemon you install separately on your machines may make a minimal telemetry request. After a successful delivery it waits 24 hours before another; an unsuccessful delivery can retry at the next scheduled check. The payload contains its version, platform, uptime in whole hours, coarse hook-event counters from the last seven days split by a fixed severity list, and a random install id generated once. That is the whole payload list. It carries no commands, keystrokes, terminal or screen content, hostnames, or strings derived from your content.

The install id distinguishes one installation's heartbeats; it is not tied to an account or user profile. Like any request to an internet service, its receiving service may process connection metadata such as an IP address; that metadata is not a field in the heartbeat payload. It exists so we can tell which versions and platforms are actually alive out there. If you would rather it sent nothing at all, turn it off with MESHD_TELEMETRY=off in the daemon's environment — the daemon works identically either way. The apps themselves never send a heartbeat.

The other exception: push notifications

If you turn on notifications, your device registers with Apple Push Notification service and gives that push token to your own machine, which stores it locally in ~/.mesh/. When an agent on that machine needs you, the machine signs a request to Apple's servers and Apple delivers the alert to your phone. Watch delivery depends on Apple's notification routing and the current beta.

This means the alert text — typically the machine name, the agent session name, and the line the agent is asking about — transits Apple's infrastructure, the same way every iOS push notification does. It is encrypted in transit and we never see it, but it is not end-to-end private from Apple. If that matters for what your agents work on, leave notifications off and open the app to check waiting agents directly.

What stays on your devices

  • Paired machine names, addresses and access tokens.
  • Your preferences, and the last snapshot of your mesh so the watch face has something to show before it reaches the network.

Deleting the app deletes all of it. To revoke a phone's access from the other side, run mesh token rotate on the machine. That locks the app out of the machine and forgets every device registered to receive notifications from it, so a phone you have revoked stops receiving them too. Your own phone re-registers the next time you open the app and pair.

No accounts

There is nothing to sign up for, nothing to log into, and no password to forget. Pairing is a code your own machine shows you; the trust lives between your devices and goes no further.

What Apple collects on its own

App Store and TestFlight distribution is Apple's, not ours. Apple gives us aggregate installation counts, opt-in crash reports, and any feedback you deliberately send. We use crash reports to fix crashes and feedback to fix the thing you told us about. Apple's handling of that data is covered by Apple's privacy policy.

Children

This app is a developer tool and is not directed at children under 13.

Changes

If any of this ever changes — in particular if a hosted relay is ever added, which would be a real change to everything above — the change will be described here and called out in the app's release notes before it ships, not after.
